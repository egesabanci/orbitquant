# P1.6 — Adaptive Bit Allocation Across Layers and Heads

**Status:** Implemented and benchmarked (pure NumPy, no model/GPU).
**Files:** `baseline/p16_adaptive.py`, `baseline/run_p16.py`
**Run:** `python3 -m baseline.run_p16 --d 64 --nrot 2000`

## Purpose

Once per-layer/per-head error measurements exist, allocate bits where they buy
the most quality under a fixed total budget: a rate-distortion scheduler over
existing quantizers rather than a new quantizer. This is the statistical
precursor: does variance-driven per-coordinate allocation beat uniform at the
same budget?

## Method

Calibration measures per-coordinate variance of rotated e1 over independent
Haar rotations; a measured rate-distortion curve D_rel(b) (exact Beta Lloyd-Max
per-unit-variance distortion) drives greedy water-filling allocation minimizing
sum_j var_j·D_rel(b_j) under a fixed budget. Compared against uniform allocation
at the same budget.

## Results — d=64, nrot=2000, budget=128, seed=0

```
per-coordinate variance: mean 0.015624 (1/d), min 0.014308, max 0.017780,
                         ratio max/min 1.243
adaptive bit distribution: 2 bits x 64 (flat)
uniform MSE  (b=2 x 64): 0.114366
adaptive MSE (budget 128):   0.114440
relative gain: +0.0650% (adaptive ~= uniform)

synthetic outlier profile (mechanism check, no rotation): 4 coords at 10x
variance, rest 1x, budget=128
  adaptive bit distribution: 1 bits x 4, 2 bits x 56, 3 bits x 4
  uniform MSE  (b=2): 73.401310
  adaptive MSE (budget 128): 70.253341
  relative gain: -4.2887% (adaptive better)
```

## Findings

1. **Under Haar rotations, adaptive allocation degenerates to uniform.**
   All rotated coordinates share the exact same marginal law (variance 1/d —
   the near-exchangeability that TurboQuant's rotation guarantees). The
   variance-driven allocator sees no coordinate to favor, so the allocation is
   flat (2 bits × 64) and MSE is identical to uniform within Monte Carlo noise
   (+0.065%). This is a *measured confirmation* of TurboQuant's design, not a
   negative result: the rotation already equalizes coordinates, so
   variance-driven allocation has nothing to exploit.

2. **The mechanism works when variance is unequal.** On a synthetic profile
   with 4 coordinates at 10× variance, adaptive allocation puts 3 bits on the
   outliers and beats uniform by 4.3% at the same budget. So the allocator is
   correct; the Haar-rotated distribution simply has no variance imbalance.

3. **Implication for the real proposal:** per-layer/head adaptive bit
   allocation (P1.6) must be driven by *measured error across layers/heads*
   (which do differ), not by within-vector coordinate variance (which Haar
   equalizes). The statistical harness confirms the allocator works; the
   layer/head dimension needs a model (P0.4, deferred).

## Implications

- Variance-driven within-vector allocation is a no-op under Haar rotation —
  expected, since the rotation's whole purpose is to equalize coordinates.
- The allocator machinery (greedy water-filling over a measured D_rel curve) is
  validated and reusable for the layer/head-level allocation once real KV
  statistics exist (GPU box, P0.4).
- Combined with P1.2 (key/value budgets) and P1.1 (mode selection), P1.6 is the
  third axis of the mixed-precision policy; all three are now benchmarked.
