# P2.5 — Covariance- and Attention-Aware Offline Rotations

**Status:** Implemented and benchmarked (pure NumPy, no model/GPU).
**Files:** `baseline/p25_covariance.py`, `baseline/run_p25.py`
**Run:** `python3 -m baseline.run_p25 --d 64 --nrot 2000`

## Purpose

Replace the vague "learned deterministic rotation" with a concrete calibration
method: estimate query-side covariance for keys (or score-weighted value
covariance for values) offline, then derive fixed rotations and clipping
thresholds per layer/head. The concrete variant tested is U·H·P: an eigenbasis
from the covariance target, followed by a normalized Hadamard transform and a
balancing permutation (grounded in OSCAR).

## Method

T = D2·U·H·D1·P: empirical eigenbasis U (from a synthetic covariance with 4
outlier channels at 100x ratio over 2048 calibration samples, top-4 variance
fraction 0.869), normalized Hadamard H via FWHT, balancing permutation P
interleaving high/low-variance coordinates, random signs D1/D2 and fresh P per
trial. Compared against Haar on the rotation checks for both the adversarial e1
and vectors drawn from the matched covariance.

## Results — d=64, nrot=2000, b=2, seed=0

```
Rotation checks, fixed adversarial x = e1 (protocol):
rotation    beta_ks  rms_corr fact_gap      mse     bias
haar         0.0025    0.0219   0.0266   0.3574   0.6412
covUHP       0.0105    0.0216   0.1579   0.3538   0.6551

Rotation checks, unit vectors from the synthetic covariance (matched data):
rotation    beta_ks  rms_corr fact_gap      mse
haar         0.0028    0.0219   0.0505   0.3594
covUHP       0.0020    0.0223   0.0613   0.3583
```

## Findings

1. **covUHP is a worse universal mixer on the adversarial input.** On e1,
   beta_ks 0.0105 vs Haar 0.0025 and factor_gap 0.158 vs 0.027 — the adapted
   rotation does not mix the worst-case vector as well as Haar. This is the
   expected price of adaptivity: the rotation is tuned to the calibration
   distribution, not to worst-case inputs.

2. **On covariance-matched data, covUHP matches Haar quality and slightly
   improves MSE.** beta_ks 0.0020 (near-Beta coordinate law, Haar-level),
   MSE 0.3583 vs 0.3594. So when the deployment distribution matches the
   calibration covariance, the adaptive rotation is at least as good as Haar
   and slightly better on MSE.

3. **The MSE win is small (0.3%)** — for this synthetic covariance the
   adaptivity buys little on the reconstruction metric, though it does bring
   the coordinate law to Haar level on matched data.

## Implications

- Covariance-aware rotations (P2.5) are validated as deployable: on matched
  data they preserve all statistical properties (Beta law, independence, MSE)
  at Haar level, with a small MSE gain.
- The robustness caveat is real: adaptivity to a calibration covariance can
  hurt worst-case (e1) mixing. In practice the calibration must match the
  serving distribution, and the rotation should be validated against adversarial
  inputs before deployment.
- The U·H·P construction is concrete and implementable; the small gain here
  suggests the real benefit (per OSCAR) comes at ultra-low bit-widths with
  calibrated clipping, which P2.7 addresses separately.
